Healing after a breakup — a guide for when it really hurts

Why breakups can feel like grief, and what actually helps you move through it 8 min read

If you're reading this, you're probably hurting. And you might also be wondering why it hurts this much — wondering if you're being dramatic, if you should be over it by now, if something is wrong with you for not being fine.

You're not being dramatic. What you're experiencing is grief.

"You don't get over a loss. You get through it — and eventually, you get used to a world that has changed."

Why breakups feel like grief

When a relationship ends, you don't just lose a person. You lose a shared future you had imagined. You lose a version of yourself — the one who was in that relationship. You lose routines, references, inside jokes, a sense of being known.

Neuroscience backs this up: studies show that the pain of social rejection activates the same brain regions as physical pain. The ache you feel is not metaphorical. It is real, and your brain is experiencing it as a genuine threat to your wellbeing.

What makes it harder than it needs to be

Our culture is not good at breakup grief. There's an invisible timeline — you should be fine in a few weeks. There's a tendency to pathologise sadness: if you're still upset after a month, something must be wrong with you. And social media makes it worse: their life appears to be going on just fine, and yours feels like it's standing still.

None of these cultural messages are true. Grief has no schedule.

What doesn't help (but feels like it should)

  • Checking their social media. Every glimpse reopens the wound before it has a chance to close.
  • Replaying the relationship over and over. Looking for the exact moment it went wrong won't give you closure — it will keep you stuck.
  • Trying to fast-forward the grief by immediately dating someone new, staying constantly busy, or pretending you're fine.

What actually helps

Feel it without drowning in it. Grief needs to be felt to move through. But there's a difference between allowing yourself to cry and ruminating for hours. Give yourself time to feel, and then gently redirect.

Create distance. If possible, take a break from contact. This isn't about punishing them — it's about giving your nervous system the space it needs to begin to regulate.

Reconnect with yourself. Relationships often require us to compromise parts of who we are. Use this time to rediscover what you actually enjoy, what you want, who you are outside of the relationship.

Let people in. Grief shared is grief halved. You don't have to be entertaining or put-together. Just let someone sit with you in it.

A note about time

Healing is not linear. There will be days that feel fine and days that feel like you're back at the beginning. This is normal. It doesn't mean you're not healing — it means you're human.

When the pain is acute — try this

Put your hand on your heart. Say out loud: "This hurts. I'm allowed to hurt. This pain means I loved something real." Then take three slow breaths. You don't have to fix anything right now.

Journaling questions
  • What do I miss most — and is it the person, or the feeling of being in that relationship?
  • What parts of myself did I set aside in this relationship that I'd like to reclaim?
  • What would I want a close friend to say to me right now? Can I say that to myself?

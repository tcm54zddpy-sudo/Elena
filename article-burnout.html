What burnout actually feels like — and how to know if you have it

Burnout isn't just being tired. Here's how to recognise it and what to do next. 7 min read

You used to care about your work. Now you drag yourself through the day feeling empty, detached, and so exhausted that even the things that used to restore you — sleep, weekends, holidays — don't seem to help. You wonder if you've just become lazy, or ungrateful, or weak.

You're probably burnt out.

"Burnout is not a badge of honour. It is the body's final way of saying: something has to change."

Burnout vs. being tired

Everyone gets tired. Rest fixes tiredness. Burnout is different — it's a state of chronic depletion that rest alone cannot undo. If you've had a full night's sleep and still feel like you have nothing left, that's an important signal.

The three signs of burnout

Research by psychologist Christina Maslach identifies three core dimensions:

  • Exhaustion — a profound physical and emotional depletion that doesn't lift with rest.
  • Cynicism (or detachment) — feeling distant from your work, going through the motions, losing the ability to care.
  • Reduced effectiveness — a decline in your ability to do your work well, accompanied by a growing sense that nothing you do matters.

You don't need all three to be burning out. Exhaustion alone, sustained over months, is enough to warrant taking it seriously.

What it actually feels like day to day

Burnout is often hard to recognise because it creeps up gradually. Some common experiences: forgetting simple things, finding it hard to concentrate, feeling irritable or numb rather than sad, dreading Sunday evenings, losing interest in things outside of work, getting sick more often.

Many people experiencing burnout don't feel dramatic distress — they feel hollow. A quiet flatness where enthusiasm used to live.

Why high achievers are especially vulnerable

Burnout doesn't only happen to people who work too hard. It happens to people who care deeply, who find meaning in what they do, who are unable to say no, who tie their self-worth to their output. The more invested you are, the more you have to lose — and the more a dysfunctional environment can take from you.

What caused it — and why it matters

Burnout is most often caused by systemic factors: unmanageable workload, lack of control, unfairness, poor community, insufficient reward. It is rarely caused by individual weakness. Understanding the source matters because the solution has to match the cause — personal coping strategies cannot fix a broken working environment.

Recovery takes longer than you think

Recovery from burnout is measured in months, not days. It requires genuine rest, reducing demands where possible, rebuilding physical health, and — often — examining what got you here and whether you want to return to the same conditions.

A starting point

If you're in burnout: the first step is not to push through. It is to stop, assess honestly, and talk to someone — a GP, a manager you trust, or a therapist. You are not being weak. You are being accurate about your state.

Journaling questions
  • When did I last feel genuinely energised by my work — and what was different then?
  • What part of this situation is within my control, and what isn't?
  • What would I need — in terms of time, rest, or change — to feel like myself again?

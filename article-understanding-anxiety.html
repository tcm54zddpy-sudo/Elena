Understanding anxiety — what it is and why it happens

A plain-language guide for people who've never looked into it before 6 min read

Anxiety is one of the most common human experiences — and one of the most misunderstood. It's not a character flaw or a sign that something is permanently wrong with you. It's a built-in alarm system that has simply gone off at the wrong time.

"Anxiety is the price of intelligence. It's what happens when a brain that can imagine the future runs ahead of the present moment."

What anxiety actually is

At its core, anxiety is your nervous system preparing you for a threat. Your body releases adrenaline, your heart beats faster, your muscles tense, your breathing becomes shallow. This is the fight-or-flight response — and it exists because, for most of human history, threats were physical and immediate.

The problem is that modern threats — a difficult conversation, a job interview, a relationship worry — are not something you can fight or run away from. But your body doesn't know that. It responds the same way to a difficult email as it would to a predator.

Why anxiety feels physical

Many people are surprised to learn that anxiety lives in the body as much as in the mind. Racing heart, tight chest, nausea, dizziness, trembling, difficulty breathing — these are all physical symptoms of anxiety, not signs that something is medically wrong.

This happens because the brain and body are in constant communication. When your brain detects a threat (even an imagined one), it floods the body with stress hormones. The body then sends signals back to the brain confirming that yes, something is very wrong — creating a feedback loop.

The anxiety spiral

One of the most distressing things about anxiety is that it can become self-reinforcing. You feel anxious about a situation, which makes you avoid it, which makes the situation feel more threatening, which makes you more anxious. This is the anxiety spiral — and it's very common.

Avoidance provides short-term relief but makes anxiety stronger over time. The antidote is gentle, gradual exposure — not forcing yourself into the deep end, but taking small steps toward what you fear.

When does anxiety become a disorder?

Anxiety becomes a clinical concern when it is persistent, disproportionate to the situation, and begins to interfere with daily life. Common anxiety disorders include generalised anxiety disorder (GAD), social anxiety, panic disorder, and specific phobias.

If anxiety is significantly affecting your sleep, relationships, work, or daily functioning, speaking to a GP or mental health professional is a worthwhile step.

What actually helps

Evidence shows that the most effective approaches to anxiety combine understanding it (so it feels less threatening), breathing and grounding techniques (to interrupt the stress response), and gradual exposure (to rebuild confidence in avoided situations). Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is the most well-researched psychological treatment for anxiety.

When anxiety spikes — try this

Place one hand on your chest and breathe out slowly until your lungs are empty. Then breathe in naturally. Repeat four times. Slow exhalation activates the parasympathetic nervous system and signals to your body that you are safe.

Journaling questions
  • When does anxiety tend to show up for me — what situations, times of day, or feelings trigger it?
  • What does my body do when I'm anxious? Where do I feel it physically?
  • Is there something I've been avoiding because of anxiety? What would one small step toward it look like?
